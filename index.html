My research

My research sits at the intersection of STEM education, technology, and equity. I study how teachers and students from underrepresented communities experience STEM, and how technology can widen access to it.

I want this work to do more than produce publications. The aim is practical support, so that every learner has access to the same quality of education regardless of their background.

I work across qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods traditions. Much of my work uses narrative inquiry and QuantCrit, a critical approach to quantitative research that asks what numbers leave out and whose interests they serve.

Current project. I am leading an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study of how pre-service STEAM teachers develop epistemic agency, professional judgment, and critical AI literacy as they work with generative AI. The project is funded by the SSHRC Canada Impact+ Research Training Award (2026 to 2028).

My path

I am a first-generation scholar from Indonesia. I began teaching mathematics in 2009, in high schools and learning centres, helping students prepare for national examinations and university entry. I then earned a Master of Education in Mathematics Education at Universitas Negeri Surabaya.

From 2015 to 2021 I taught statistics, research methods, and mathematical economics at Universitas Pamulang, where I received the Best Lecturer award in 2018. In 2021 I received a Fulbright scholarship to pursue a Ph.D. at The Ohio State University under the guidance of Dr. Theodore Chao. My dissertation, Voices from the Margins, explored digital storytelling in mathematics education with first-generation pre-service teachers from rural and low-income backgrounds.

Before joining Ontario Tech, I served as Fellowship Advisor and Honors College instructor at Florida Gulf Coast University, where I helped students prepare competitive fellowship applications for awards such as Fulbright.

Beyond the university

I founded Rumah Cahaya Empat Ribu (RuMAER), a foundation that offers free professional development to teachers in rural and remote areas. I also serve as editor in some journals and mentor early-career researchers through the Youth Center for Research.

Who Gets to Study STEM? What 16,627 Fulbright Students Reveal

September 24, 2026

As a Fulbright Scholar with a PhD in STEM education, I was curious about which countries get to send students to study STEM under the Fulbright Foreign Student Program. Here is something interesting we found from the data (16,627 students from 161 countries):

1) There is one country that sent more than 10 times the typical median country's count. One country basically dominates the leaderboard.

2) The top 10 countries out of 161 total grab 41–50% of ALL the spots every single year. So, less than 7% of countries take up nearly half of all the opportunities.

3) In 2017, it was even more interesting, the top 10 countries took over half (50.3%) of all STEM spots that year.

4) The surprising plot twist: even though wealthy countries send the most students overall, low-income countries have the HIGHEST proportion of their Fulbright students going into STEM (52%), higher than high-income countries (36%).

5) When a student from a lower-income country does get selected, they are disproportionately likely to choose STEM, possibly because a STEM degree is seen as the highest-value ticket to future opportunity.

An Undergrad Just Published in a High-Ranking Journal, Before Even Writing His Thesis

August 18, 2026

This article was written not by someone with a doctorate, not by a PhD student, not by a master's student. It was written by an undergrad student, someone who has not even graduated from college yet (and has not even started his undergrad thesis), but he can manage to get his very first article accepted as first author in a high-ranking journal from a reputable publisher.

This student is Gabriel Kronbach, one of my students (but technically he never enrolled in my class) from a university in Florida.

The editor and the reviewers were very impressed with his work, and he received only very minor revisions (that was the first time I ever received very minor revisions from a reputable publisher). That is why the time from submission to acceptance was less than 2 months.

Here is what we found from this project:

1) AI is not inherently good or bad for meaning. It is actually a mirror. It reflects the orientation you bring to it. Use it as a thinking partner and engage critically, and meaning tends to grow. Let it do your thinking for you, and meaning tends to shrink. We call this pattern existential outsourcing.

2) Companion AI helps most in the middle. AI companionship boosted wellbeing most for people who already had moderate social connection (not the most isolated, and not the most connected).

3) How you prompt AI might matter more than how often you use it. People who treated AI as a collaborator (not a shortcut) showed dramatically different psychological outcomes than people who simply outsourced their thinking to it.
